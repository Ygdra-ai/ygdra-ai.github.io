Competitive advantage levels of AI

In short

A company can use AI at three levels. Level 1: a general model works out the business afresh from whatever data it is shown. Level 2: the AI its system vendors sell, which anyone can buy. Level 3: agents work from the company’s own understanding, kept in a record it owns. Only level 3 puts a company ahead.

01

Level 1 · General AI on your data

A general model is given access to some of the company’s data and asked questions. It is fast to start and often impressive. But the understanding of what the data means is rebuilt by the model every time, from whatever it happens to see. Two engineers asking the same question with different prompts, or the same engineer a model version later, get different answers. Nothing the model works out is kept in a form the company owns. Your people use it every day, and should. It is a good assistant and a poor foundation.

02

Level 2 · AI built into your systems

The PLM, ERP and design-tool vendors build AI into their products, on their own business logic. This solves real entry-level problems, and a company should use it where it fits. Its limit is not quality but availability: anyone can buy it, and the same logic is sold to every customer, including the competitors. Spending more on the latest release keeps a company even with them. It cannot put it ahead.

03

Level 3 · AI on what only you know

Every company that engineers and builds to order knows things its competitors do not: why a variant is configured the way it is, which supplier part is trusted for which load, which check catches the costly error, how an order really moves from sale to delivery. Some of it is in documents and data, much of it is in a few people’s heads. Level 3 writes that understanding down, with the people who hold it and under their roles, into the company’s own record with its sources, and lets agents work from it through one controlled door. The people stay its authors; the record makes their knowledge count for more, not less.

Level 3 records the understanding a company has; it does not invent one. Where the best knowledge sits in a few heads, level 3 is also how it stops leaving the company with them.

Why it matters

This is the only level a competitor cannot buy, because it is not for sale.

04

The levels build on each other

Level 3 does not replace levels 1 and 2. The general models stay useful, and the vendors’ AI keeps doing what it does inside their systems. The record sits beside those systems, as it sits beside the data in them, and makes every agent that may use it better informed. A company does not choose a level; it decides whether its own understanding is part of its AI or not.

05

What level 3 takes

Two kinds of understanding, together. One is of the business: how build-to-order manufacturers sell, engineer, buy, build and deliver, and what their organisations look like. The other is of how engineering, manufacturing and project workflows change when agents take part: which steps fall away, which move to agents, where a person must decide and sign. The first alone produces good process maps and no change. The second alone produces agents that work in a demo and not on the shop floor.

06

Where the idea comes from

The levels apply an established idea in strategy, the resource-based view: a resource gives lasting advantage only when it is valuable, rare and hard to imitate (Barney, Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage, Journal of Management 17(1), 1991). What every competitor can buy keeps a company, at best, even with them. The same argument was made about information technology in general (Carr, IT Doesn’t Matter, Harvard Business Review, May 2003). Getting knowledge out of people’s heads into explicit form is what knowledge-management research calls externalisation (Nonaka and Takeuchi, The Knowledge-Creating Company, 1995).
